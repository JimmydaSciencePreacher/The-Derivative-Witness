# FW-001 — Before Christianity and the Earliest Christian Writers

**Investigation question:** Did the idea of free will originate within Christianity, and how did the earliest Christian writers discuss human choice, responsibility, and freedom?

## Finding so far

The philosophical questions later grouped under “free will” did not originate within Christianity. Ancient Greek and Roman writers had already discussed choice, voluntary action, responsibility, fate, and what is within human control. The Latin expression *libera voluntas* is attested before Christianity. These earlier discussions should not automatically be equated with later Christian or modern doctrines.

The earliest Christian evidence also needs to be read in stages. Early moral instruction about choosing life, obeying God, and being accountable is not necessarily a developed theory of free will. More explicit arguments about freedom, fate, foreknowledge, and responsibility appear in second-century Christian writers.

## Evidence: pre-Christian background

1. Plato and Aristotle discussed agency, voluntary action, choice, and responsibility. Their concepts should not simply be translated into the later phrase “free will.”
2. Hellenistic schools, including Stoics and Epicureans, debated fate, causation, and what is “up to us.”
3. The Latin expression *libera voluntas* is attested before Christianity, including in the writings of Lucretius and Cicero.
4. Terminology and doctrine developed over time; a first occurrence of a phrase is not automatically the origin of every idea later associated with it.

## Evidence: earliest Christian writers

The following sequence distinguishes early ethical instruction from explicit argument about freedom. Dates are approximate and disputed in some cases.

### 1. The Didache — late first to early second century

The *Didache* opens with the “Two Ways”: the way of life and the way of death. It gives moral instruction and commands for a Christian community. Its opening material draws on an earlier Jewish moral tradition. It witnesses to a framework of moral direction and response, but it does not present a developed philosophical doctrine of free will.

Sources:
- [Cambridge Edition of Early Christian Writings — Didache](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-edition-of-early-christian-writings/didache/F6EEA99D2B1AB5A25464F0C619B86021)
- [Oxford Bibliographies — Didache](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62341/reference-article-abstract/554099864)

### 2. Clement of Rome — First Clement, approximately 96 CE

First Clement addresses division, jealousy, obedience, humility, and righteousness in the church at Corinth. It assumes moral responsibility and exhorts its readers to faithful conduct. It does not set out a systematic account of free will. This is evidence of early Christian moral exhortation, not by itself evidence of philosophical borrowing.

### 3. Ignatius of Antioch — approximately 98–117 CE

Ignatius's seven letters emphasize faithfulness, unity, obedience, Christian conduct, and the meaning of his approaching martyrdom. They provide evidence of early Christian ethical and ecclesial concerns, but are not a systematic treatment of human freedom.

Source:
- [Cambridge Edition of Early Christian Writings — Ignatius of Antioch, Letters](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-edition-of-early-christian-writings/ignatius-of-antioch-letters-selections/D58C2FA5E4FF0C60A93DFDCCD30D510A)

### 4. Polycarp of Smyrna — early to mid-second century

Polycarp's Letter to the Philippians exhorts its readers to righteousness, patience, faith, and obedience. It is important evidence for the ethical instruction of the early church, but it should not be cited as though it contains a developed free-will argument.

Primary text:
- [Polycarp, Letter to the Philippians](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0136.htm)

### 5. The Shepherd of Hermas — commonly placed in the first half to middle of the second century

In Commandment 12, Hermas asks whether God's commandments can be kept because they seem exceedingly difficult. The answer emphasizes the person's disposition and willingness to obey. The work discusses desire, repentance, conduct, and moral capacity. It is a stronger witness to the developing discussion of human response, though it should not be made to carry every later meaning of “free will.”

Primary text:
- [The Shepherd of Hermas, Book II, Commandment 12](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02012.htm)

### 6. Justin Martyr — approximately 100–165 CE

Justin's *First Apology*, chapters 43–44, explicitly addresses fate, divine foreknowledge, choice, and moral accountability. He argues that if human actions occurred by unavoidable fate, praise and blame would lose their basis. He appeals to prophetic teaching about choosing good and rejecting evil.

This is a clear early Christian argument for human freedom and responsibility in response to the problem of fate and foreknowledge.

Primary text:
- [Justin Martyr, First Apology, chapters 43–44](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0126.htm)

### 7. Tatian — second century

Tatian belongs in this investigation because scholarship identifies him as an important early witness in the terminology of freedom of the will. Michael Frede's study describes Tatian as the first writer to use the expression “freedom of the will.” That claim concerns the history of a particular expression, not necessarily the first Christian discussion of choice or responsibility.

This requires a closer primary-text and Greek-terminology check before the exact priority claim is treated as settled.

Scholarly lead:
- [Michael Frede, “An Early Christian View on a Free Will: Origen”](https://doi.org/10.1525/california/9780520268487.003.0007)

### 8. Theophilus of Antioch — late second century

In *To Autolycus*, Book II, chapter 27, Theophilus states that God made man free and with power over himself. He connects obedience and disobedience with life, death, and the human condition. This is an explicit statement about human freedom, not merely a general exhortation to behave well.

Primary text:
- [Theophilus, To Autolycus, Book II, chapter 27](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02042.htm)

### 9. Irenaeus of Lyons — approximately 130–202 CE

In *Against Heresies*, Book IV, chapters 37–39, Irenaeus gives a substantial theological account of human freedom. Chapter 37 says that God made human beings free and placed in them the power of choice. He argues that obedience is voluntary rather than compelled and relates human choice to responsibility and judgment. He draws on Matthew 23:37 and other biblical passages.

Primary texts:
- [Irenaeus, Against Heresies, Book IV, chapter 37](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103437.htm)
- [Christian Classics Ethereal Library — Against Heresies IV](https://ccel.org/ccel/irenaeus/against_heresies_iv/anf01.ix.vi.xxxviii.html)

### 10. Clement of Alexandria and Tertullian — late second to early third century

Clement of Alexandria discusses free choice, grace, willing, action, and responsibility in the *Stromata*. He explicitly holds together human choice and the need for divine grace; his account should not be reduced to the claim that salvation depends on unaided human effort.

Primary texts:
- [Clement, Stromata, Book II](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02102.htm)
- [Clement, Stromata, Book VI](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02106.htm)

Tertullian's *A Treatise on the Soul*, chapters 21–22, discusses the soul's freedom of will and its mutable dispositions. This is another explicit development of the discussion.

Primary text:
- [Tertullian, A Treatise on the Soul](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0310.htm)

### 11. Origen — third century

Origen's *On First Principles* contains a more extensive and systematic Christian exploration of freedom, rational creatures, moral responsibility, and divine judgment. Scholarship treats Origen as a major developer of the subject, while also noting that later writers, including Augustine, developed the discussion in different directions.

Scholarly source:
- [Michael Frede, “An Early Christian View on a Free Will: Origen,” pages 102–124](https://doi.org/10.1525/california/9780520268487.003.0007)

## What the evidence establishes

1. Christianity did not originate the broad philosophical questions about choice, responsibility, fate, and freedom.
2. The earliest Christian writings include moral instruction and assumptions of responsibility before there is a fully developed Christian argument about free will.
3. Justin Martyr provides a clear second-century argument connecting freedom, foreknowledge, fate, and moral accountability.
4. Tatian is an important lead in the history of the exact terminology; the claim about the earliest use of the expression requires verification against the original language and primary text.
5. Theophilus, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, and Tertullian provide explicit further discussions. Origen develops a more systematic treatment.
6. The evidence shows development and diversity. It does not establish that all early Christian writers held one uniform doctrine.

## What this evidence does not establish

- It does not prove that Christian writers simply copied a particular Greek philosophical school.
- Similar vocabulary or shared questions do not prove direct borrowing.
- It does not show that the earliest Christian writers meant exactly what modern theologians mean by “free will.”
- It does not settle the biblical question of what kind of will, capacity, or authority was given to Adam.
- It does not settle the relationship between human willingness, divine initiative, grace, and salvation.

## Historical interpretation and open questions

A useful distinction has emerged:

- **Moral exhortation:** commands, warnings, and calls to obedience.
- **Moral responsibility:** the assumption that actions can rightly be praised or blamed.
- **Human capacity:** claims that a person can obey or disobey.
- **Freedom terminology:** the words used for freedom, self-determination, and free choice.
- **Developed doctrine:** a systematic account connecting freedom with God, grace, foreknowledge, sin, and judgment.

These categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. The investigation must not treat the earliest occurrence of one category as proof that all the others were already present.

## Triple Constraint review

### 1. Biblical compliance

**Status: Open for theological evaluation.**

The historical record identifies what Christian writers argued. It does not establish that their arguments accurately represent the whole biblical teaching. The relevant comparison with the literal KJV Genesis 1–2 foundation remains to be made without forcing the historical evidence into a predetermined conclusion.

### 2. Scientific coherence

**Status: Historical-method review; no empirical-science conclusion claimed.**

This investigation concerns historical documents, language, chronology, and interpretation rather than a physical or biological hypothesis. The claims are kept within what surviving texts and historical scholarship can support. Historical evidence must not be mislabeled as experimental scientific confirmation.

### 3. Consistency with GUA-W

**Status: Open; no architectural placement imposed.**

The distinction between human capacity to choose and authority to determine good and evil must remain visible. The historical writers' use of freedom language does not, by itself, resolve that distinction. GUA-W is not revised to accommodate the writers; their claims remain historical material to be examined against its foundational source.

**Overall status:** Substantial historical progress; theological and GUA-W evaluation remain open.

## Four witness reflections

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

How does the literal KJV Genesis 1–2 distinguish the human capacity to see, desire, respond, and act from the authority to define good and evil? How should later Christian claims be examined without allowing later terminology to determine the meaning of the foundational text?

These questions remain open.

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

No new GUA-W structure or doctrine is established by this historical investigation. The material reinforces the need to distinguish capacity, authority, willingness, action, and responsibility rather than treating them as interchangeable.

This is a question for comparison with the foundation, not a conclusion imposed upon it.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The historical record shows a progression from moral instruction and responsibility to explicit arguments about freedom, fate, foreknowledge, and judgment. The earliest Christian writers were not a single undifferentiated voice. Their terminology, arguments, and theological emphases require examination individually.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A responsible witness must distinguish what a source says from what later interpreters make it mean. The first appearance of a term, the first discussion of a related idea, and the development of a doctrine are different historical questions. A witness must preserve those distinctions and leave unsupported conclusions open.
