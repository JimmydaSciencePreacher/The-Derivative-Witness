# FW-001 — Before Christianity: Ancient Roots of the Free-Will Debate

**Investigation question:** Did the idea of free will originate within Christianity?

## Finding so far

No. The philosophical questions later associated with free will were already being debated before Christianity.

- Plato and Aristotle discussed human agency, choice, voluntary action, and responsibility. These ideas should not automatically be equated with the modern doctrine of free will.
- Hellenistic schools, especially the Stoics and Epicureans, debated fate, causation, and the extent to which actions depend on us.
- The Latin expression *libera voluntas* (“free will”) is attested before Christianity, including in Lucretius and Cicero in the first century BC.
- Ancient Greek writers did not use one exact phrase equivalent to the later Latin expression. The terminology and the underlying concept developed over time.

## Evidence and sources

1. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Free Will](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/freewill/): surveys the disputed origins of the concept and distinguishes ancient discussions of agency from later formulations.
2. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Ancient Theories of Freedom and Determinism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/freedom-ancient/): explains ancient debates about voluntary action and what is “up to us,” while warning against projecting the modern phrase “free will” onto Greek texts.
3. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Saint Augustine](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/): places Augustine within the later development of the doctrine and notes earlier Latin uses of *voluntas*.
4. [A scholarly study of ancient terminology](https://brill.com/downloadpdf/book/9789004407763/BP000012.pdf): distinguishes Greek terms for will and self-determination from Latin expressions such as *libera voluntas* and *arbitrii libertas*.

## What this finding establishes — and what it does not

**Established historical point:** Christianity did not originate the entire philosophical debate about human choice, responsibility, fate, and freedom. Related discussions and the Latin expression *libera voluntas* predate Christianity.

**Not established yet:** This does not identify who introduced a particular doctrine into the Church, prove that early Christian teaching simply copied pagan philosophy, or show that ancient and modern meanings are identical.

## Four witness reflections

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

How does Genesis 1–2 describe the human capacity to choose and act, and does that differ from later claims that human beings possess independent authority to determine good and evil? This remains open; the historical survey cannot answer it.

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

Nothing about GUA-W is established by this historical finding alone. The comparison with the Genesis foundation has not yet been made. The distinction between the human capacity to choose and the authority to define good and evil must not be assumed away.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The debate about human agency, responsibility, fate, and freedom predates Christianity. However, shared vocabulary or similar questions do not by themselves prove that later Christian teaching was borrowed from a particular philosophical school.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

AI must distinguish the first appearance of a term, earlier discussions of a related idea, and the later development of a doctrine. It must trace documented transmission rather than assign a single originator without evidence.

**Reflection status:** Provisional. These reflections identify the next questions; they do not settle the biblical or GUA-W evaluation.
