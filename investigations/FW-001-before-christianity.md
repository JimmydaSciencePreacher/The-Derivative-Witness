# FW-001 — Before Christianity and the Earliest Christian Writers

**Investigation question:** Did the idea of free will originate within Christianity, and how did the earliest Christian writers discuss human choice, responsibility, and freedom?

## Finding so far

The philosophical questions later grouped under “free will” did not originate within Christianity. Ancient Greek and Roman writers had already discussed choice, voluntary action, responsibility, fate, and what is within human control. The Latin expression *libera voluntas* is attested before Christianity. These earlier discussions should not automatically be equated with later Christian or modern doctrines.

The earliest Christian evidence also needs to be read in stages. Early moral instruction about choosing life, obeying God, and being accountable is not necessarily a developed theory of free will. More explicit arguments about freedom, fate, foreknowledge, and responsibility appear in second-century Christian writers.

## Evidence: pre-Christian background

1. Plato and Aristotle discussed agency, voluntary action, choice, and responsibility. Their concepts should not simply be translated into the later phrase “free will.”
2. Hellenistic schools, including Stoics and Epicureans, debated fate, causation, and what is “up to us.”
3. The Latin expression *libera voluntas* is attested before Christianity, including in the writings of Lucretius and Cicero.
4. Terminology and doctrine developed over time; a first occurrence of a phrase is not automatically the origin of every idea later associated with it.

Background scholarship:
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Free Will](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/freewill/)
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Ancient Theories of Freedom and Determinism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/freedom-ancient/)
- [A scholarly study of ancient terminology](https://brill.com/downloadpdf/book/9789004407763/BP000012.pdf)

## Evidence: earliest Christian writers

The following sequence distinguishes early ethical instruction from explicit argument about freedom. Dates are approximate and disputed in some cases.

### 1. The Didache — late first to early second century

The *Didache* opens with the “Two Ways”: the way of life and the way of death. It gives moral instruction and commands for a Christian community. Its opening material draws on an earlier Jewish moral tradition. It witnesses to a framework of moral direction and response, but it does not present a developed philosophical doctrine of free will.

Sources:
- [Cambridge Edition of Early Christian Writings — Didache](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-edition-of-early-christian-writings/didache/F6EEA99D2B1AB5A25464F0C619B86021)
- [Oxford Bibliographies — Didache](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62341/reference-article-abstract/554099864)

### 2. Clement of Rome — First Clement, approximately 96 CE

First Clement addresses division, jealousy, obedience, humility, and righteousness in the church at Corinth. It assumes moral responsibility and exhorts its readers to faithful conduct. It does not set out a systematic account of free will. This is evidence of early Christian moral exhortation, not by itself evidence of philosophical borrowing.

### 3. Ignatius of Antioch — approximately 98–117 CE

Ignatius's seven letters emphasize faithfulness, unity, obedience, Christian conduct, and the meaning of his approaching martyrdom. They provide evidence of early Christian ethical and ecclesial concerns, but are not a systematic treatment of human freedom.

Source:
- [Cambridge Edition of Early Christian Writings — Ignatius of Antioch, Letters](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-edition-of-early-christian-writings/ignatius-of-antioch-letters-selections/D58C2FA5E4FF0C60A93DFDCCD30D510A)

### 4. Polycarp of Smyrna — early to mid-second century

Polycarp's Letter to the Philippians exhorts its readers to righteousness, patience, faith, and obedience. It is important evidence for the ethical instruction of the early church, but it should not be cited as though it contains a developed free-will argument.

Primary text:
- [Polycarp, Letter to the Philippians](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0136.htm)

### 5. The Shepherd of Hermas — commonly placed in the first half to middle of the second century

In Commandment 12, Hermas asks whether God's commandments can be kept because they seem exceedingly difficult. The answer emphasizes the person's disposition and willingness to obey. The work discusses desire, repentance, conduct, and moral capacity. It is a stronger witness to the developing discussion of human response, though it should not be made to carry every later meaning of “free will.”

Primary text:
- [The Shepherd of Hermas, Book II, Commandment 12](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02012.htm)

### 6. Justin Martyr — approximately 100–165 CE

Justin's *First Apology*, chapters 43–44, explicitly addresses fate, divine foreknowledge, choice, and moral accountability. He argues that if human actions occurred by unavoidable fate, praise and blame would lose their basis. He appeals to prophetic teaching about choosing good and rejecting evil.

This is a clear early Christian argument for human freedom and responsibility in response to the problem of fate and foreknowledge.

Primary text:
- [Justin Martyr, First Apology, chapters 43–44](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0126.htm)

### 7. Tatian — second century

Tatian belongs in this investigation because scholarship identifies him as an important early witness in the terminology of freedom of the will. Michael Frede's study describes Tatian as the first writer to use the expression “freedom of the will.” That claim concerns the history of a particular expression, not necessarily the first Christian discussion of choice or responsibility.

This requires a closer primary-text and Greek-terminology check before the exact priority claim is treated as settled.

Scholarly lead:
- [Michael Frede, “An Early Christian View on a Free Will: Origen”](https://doi.org/10.1525/california/9780520268487.003.0007)

### 8. Theophilus of Antioch — late second century

In *To Autolycus*, Book II, chapter 27, Theophilus states that God made man free and with power over himself. He connects obedience and disobedience with life, death, and the human condition. This is an explicit statement about human freedom, not merely a general exhortation to behave well.

Primary text:
- [Theophilus, To Autolycus, Book II, chapter 27](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02042.htm)

### 9. Irenaeus of Lyons — approximately 130–202 CE

In *Against Heresies*, Book IV, chapters 37–39, Irenaeus gives a substantial theological account of human freedom. Chapter 37 says that God made human beings free and placed in them the power of choice. He argues that obedience is voluntary rather than compelled and relates human choice to responsibility and judgment. He draws on Matthew 23:37 and other biblical passages.

Primary texts:
- [Irenaeus, Against Heresies, Book IV, chapter 37](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103437.htm)
- [Christian Classics Ethereal Library — Against Heresies IV](https://ccel.org/ccel/irenaeus/against_heresies_iv/anf01.ix.vi.xxxviii.html)

### 10. Clement of Alexandria and Tertullian — late second to early third century

Clement of Alexandria discusses free choice, grace, willing, action, and responsibility in the *Stromata*. He explicitly holds together human choice and the need for divine grace; his account should not be reduced to the claim that salvation depends on unaided human effort.

Primary texts:
- [Clement, Stromata, Book II](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02102.htm)
- [Clement, Stromata, Book VI](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02106.htm)

Tertullian's *A Treatise on the Soul*, chapters 21–22, discusses the soul's freedom of will and its mutable dispositions. This is another explicit development of the discussion.

Primary text:
- [Tertullian, A Treatise on the Soul](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0310.htm)

### 11. Origen — third century

Origen's *On First Principles* contains a more extensive and systematic Christian exploration of freedom, rational creatures, moral responsibility, and divine judgment. Scholarship treats Origen as a major developer of the subject, while also noting that later writers, including Augustine, developed the discussion in different directions.

Scholarly source:
- [Michael Frede, “An Early Christian View on a Free Will: Origen,” pages 102–124](https://doi.org/10.1525/california/9780520268487.003.0007)

## What the evidence establishes

1. Christianity did not originate the broad philosophical questions about choice, responsibility, fate, and freedom.
2. The earliest Christian writings include moral instruction and assumptions of responsibility before there is a fully developed Christian argument about free will.
3. Justin Martyr provides a clear second-century argument connecting freedom, foreknowledge, fate, and moral accountability.
4. Tatian is an important lead in the history of the exact terminology; the claim about the earliest use of the expression requires verification against the original language and primary text.
5. Theophilus, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, and Tertullian provide explicit further discussions. Origen develops a more systematic treatment.
6. The evidence shows development and diversity. It does not establish that all early Christian writers held one uniform doctrine.

## What this evidence does not establish

- It does not prove that Christian writers simply copied a particular Greek philosophical school.
- Similar vocabulary or shared questions do not prove direct borrowing.
- It does not show that the earliest Christian writers meant exactly what modern theologians mean by “free will.”
- It does not settle the biblical question of what kind of will, capacity, or authority was given to Adam.
- It does not settle the relationship between human willingness, divine initiative, grace, and salvation.

## Historical interpretation and open questions

A useful distinction has emerged:

- **Moral exhortation:** commands, warnings, and calls to obedience.
- **Moral responsibility:** the assumption that actions can rightly be praised or blamed.
- **Human capacity:** claims that a person can obey or disobey.
- **Freedom terminology:** the words used for freedom, self-determination, and free choice.
- **Developed doctrine:** a systematic account connecting freedom with God, grace, foreknowledge, sin, and judgment.

These categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. The investigation must not treat the earliest occurrence of one category as proof that all the others were already present.

## Terminology audit — primary-text examination

This section extends the historical investigation by checking key claims against surviving primary texts and the original-language evidence where available. It does not settle the biblical or GUA-W evaluation.

### Tatian: *Address to the Greeks*, chapters 7 and 11

A Greek-English parallel edition of chapter 7 is available through the University of Fribourg's Bibliothek der Kirchenväter:
- [Tatian, *Oratio ad Graecos*, chapter 7 — Greek and English](https://bkv.unifr.ch/en/works/cpg-1104/compare/oratio-ad-graecos/8/address-of-tatian-to-the-greeks)
- [English translation, chapters 7–8 and 11](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0202.htm)

The Greek text of chapter 7 contains two significant expressions:

- **αὐτεξούσιον (*autexousion*)** — a term expressing being one's own master, having power or authority over oneself, or being self-determining. The exact English rendering depends on context.
- **ἐλευθερίᾳ τῆς προαιρέσεως (*eleutheria tēs proaireseōs*)** — literally, freedom/liberty of choice or decision.

Tatian applies this language to angels and human beings. He connects it with moral praise and punishment, and says that the Logos foreknows future events as arising not by fate but through the self-determining decisions of agents.

The text therefore connects freedom, responsibility, divine foreknowledge, and opposition to fate. It also makes a theological claim: goodness belongs to God, while human beings come to moral perfection through freedom of choice. That claim must be reported as Tatian's position, not silently adopted as the conclusion of this investigation.

Chapter 11 is headed in the surviving English translation, “The Sin of Men Due Not to Fate, But to Free-Will.” Its concluding argument says that human beings became enslaved through sin and that the evil manifested by human beings can again be rejected. The Greek text for this chapter has not yet been collated in this investigation; therefore the English phrase “free-will” is not being treated as proof of the precise Greek expression used there.

### What does “Tatian first used the expression” mean?

Michael Frede's chapter “An Early Christian View on a Free Will: Origen” states that Tatian was the first person ever to use the expression “the freedom of the will,” while identifying Origen as a major developer of the concept:
- [Michael Frede, *A Free Will: Origins of the Notion in Ancient Thought*, chapter 7](https://doi.org/10.1525/california/9780520268487.003.0007)

This priority claim must be stated narrowly. It does **not** mean Tatian invented the general idea of choice, coined every Greek term associated with freedom, or originated all discussion of self-determination. The term *autexousion* is attested in earlier philosophical and Jewish-Hellenistic material, including Epictetus and Philo, as noted in scholarly discussion:
- [The Philosophy of Early Christianity — discussion of *autexousion* and *prohairesis*](https://gnosis.study/library/%D0%93%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B7%D0%B8%D1%81/%2B%20%D0%9D%D0%B5%20%D1%82%D0%BE%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BA%D0%BE%20%D0%BE%20%D0%B3%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B7%D0%B8%D1%81%D0%B5/ENG/Karamanolis%20G.%20-%20The%20Philosophy%20of%20Early%20Christianity.pdf)
- [The Routledge Handbook of Early Christian Philosophy — freedom and the power to choose](https://gnosis.study/library/%D0%93%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B7%D0%B8%D1%81/%2B%20%D0%9D%D0%B5%20%D1%82%D0%BE%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BA%D0%BE%20%D0%BE%20%D0%B3%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B7%D0%B8%D1%81%D0%B5/ENG/The%20Routledge%20Handbook%20of%20Early%20Christian%20Philosophy.pdf)

**Current assessment:** Frede's claim is a credible scholarly lead about a particular expression. The precise Greek wording and the boundaries of the priority claim still require examination of the full argument and primary-language evidence. It must not be expanded into the claim that Tatian invented *autexousion* or the idea of human choice.

### Justin Martyr: *First Apology*, chapters 43–44

The available English primary-text translation gives a clear argument:
- Chapter 43 rejects the inference that prophecy or foreknowledge makes every event occur by fatal necessity.
- Justin argues that without the power to avoid evil and choose good, human beings would not be accountable.
- Chapter 44 appeals to Moses and to Isaiah as scriptural witnesses for choosing good, repentance, and obedience.

Primary text:
- [Justin Martyr, *First Apology*, chapters 43–44](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0126.htm)

An important textual distinction is required. Justin's appeal to Moses — “Behold, before your face are good and evil: choose the good” in this English translation — appears to combine the language of Deuteronomy 30:15 and 30:19. It is not a quotation from Genesis 1–2. Justin's argument therefore uses later biblical instruction about choosing, not a direct quotation from the Garden account.

Justin also says in chapter 10 that human beings use rational faculties endowed by God to choose what pleases Him, and that God persuades and leads them to faith. This passage should be read alongside chapters 43–44 rather than reducing Justin's position to one isolated sentence.

**Open textual task:** Collate the Greek text of *First Apology* 10 and 43–44 before making claims about Justin's exact Greek terminology. The English translation alone does not settle whether his vocabulary corresponds precisely to Tatian's.

### Irenaeus: *Against Heresies*, Book IV, chapter 37

The accessible text of this passage is transmitted in a Latin-based version and English translation:
- [Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, IV.37](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103437.htm)

Irenaeus argues that God made human beings free and placed in them the power of choice. He says that good is given by God but that those who receive it must keep it; obedience is voluntary rather than compelled. He appeals to Matthew 23:37 and Romans 2:4–7.

This passage must be distinguished from a direct quotation of Irenaeus's original Greek. The surviving transmission of *Against Heresies* is substantially dependent on an ancient Latin translation, with Greek fragments and other witnesses surviving for portions of the work. The precise original Greek vocabulary should not be reconstructed from an English translation alone.

**Open textual task:** Compare the Latin wording, available Greek fragments, and critical editions before equating Irenaeus's terminology with Tatian's.

The expressions *autexousion*, *prohairesis*, *eleutheria*, and Latin *libera voluntas* must not be treated as automatic synonyms. Their usage changes by author, period, language, and argument. A translation that renders several expressions as “free will” can conceal important differences.

## Updated historical assessment

The primary-text check strengthens the finding that second-century Christian writers explicitly discussed human choice, responsibility, and freedom. It also reveals that their arguments are not interchangeable:

- Tatian places freedom within a creation account involving the Logos, angels, humanity, goodness, and foreknowledge.
- Justin argues against fatal necessity and appeals to scriptural commands to choose good.
- Irenaeus emphasizes voluntary obedience and the human responsibility to keep the good given by God.

These are related discussions, but they are not yet shown to be one uniform doctrine or one direct line of borrowing. Nor does the presence of these arguments settle what the literal KJV Genesis 1–2 establishes.

## Third examination — capacity, divine provision, and rational judgment

This stage examines Justin's original Greek, the transmitted text of Irenaeus IV.37, and Origen's *On First Principles* III.1. The central question is not merely whether these writers affirm choice, but what they say is within human power, what is given by God, and how human response relates to divine action.

### 1. Justin Martyr — *First Apology* 10 and 43–44

A Greek text of Justin's *First Apology* is available here:
- [Greek text, chapter 10](https://github.com/willf/justin-martyr/blob/main/apology-1-grk.md#10)
- [Greek text, chapter 43](https://github.com/willf/justin-martyr/blob/main/apology-1-grk.md#43)
- [English translation, chapters 43–44](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0126.htm)

Chapter 43 contains two significant expressions:

- **τὸ ἐφ’ ἡμῖν (*to eph' hēmin*)** — what is within our power, or what depends on us.
- **προαιρέσει ἐλευθέρᾳ (*proairesei eleuthera*)** — by free choice.

Justin argues that if actions occurred by fate, moral praise and blame would have no just basis. He says that the human race must have the power to avoid shameful things and choose good things if people are to be accountable for their actions.

Chapter 10 adds an important qualification to a simplistic reading of this argument. Justin says that human beings did not bring about their own original existence. God created and provided; human beings are persuaded and led toward faith through the rational faculties God bestowed. Justin therefore locates human response within capacities that are received, not self-originated.

This does not by itself settle how Justin understands grace or the origin of faith in every context. It does establish that his account in this passage does not present human beings as the source of their own existence or rational powers.

**Observation:** Justin explicitly defends free choice and moral accountability while acknowledging that the human capacity through which people respond is God-given.

**Interpretive limit:** This is Justin's argument. It is not yet a conclusion about the literal meaning of Genesis 1–2 or the GUA-W distinction between capacity and authority.

### 2. Irenaeus — *Against Heresies* IV.37

Primary text:
- [Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.37](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103437.htm)

In IV.37 Irenaeus argues that God gives what is good, while human beings may keep or reject what has been given. He describes obedience as voluntary, not compelled. He appeals to Matthew 23:37, Romans 2:4–7, and other commands and exhortations.

His argument is not simply that human beings originate goodness. In this passage, good is first given by God; the human response concerns receiving, keeping, or rejecting it.

**Textual limitation:** *Against Heresies* was composed in Greek, but the complete work is transmitted principally through an ancient Latin translation. Greek fragments and quotations survive, but they do not provide the complete Greek text of every passage. The date of the Latin translation is debated; scholarship places it variously in the third or later fourth century. The exact original Greek vocabulary of IV.37 must therefore remain unverified rather than being reverse-engineered from an English translation.

Sources on transmission:
- [Oxford University Press — Early Translations of Other Works](https://academic.oup.com/book/12425/chapter/162891141)
- [Reading Irenaeus — note on source texts and dates](https://brill.com/previewpdf/book/9789047433439/Bej.9789004166820.i-244_009.xml)
- [Concordia Seminary study discussing the surviving Irenaean languages](https://scholar.csl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1024&context=thd)

**Observation:** The transmitted text supports a distinction between the good God gives and the human response that keeps or rejects it.

**Interpretive limit:** The argument can be described from the surviving text, but exact Greek lexical comparisons with Justin and Tatian cannot yet be claimed.

### 3. Origen — *On First Principles* III.1

Primary text:
- [Origen, *On First Principles*, Book III](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04123.htm)
- [Book III.1, Greek-derived translation sections and edition notes](https://garovartabedian.com/early/library/origen-first-principles/book-iii-chapter-i-part-11)

Origen develops a more detailed account of human response.

#### A. Natural impulse and rational judgment — III.1.2–4

Origen distinguishes things moved by external causes from living things whose movement arises from within. He uses spiders weaving webs and bees forming honeycombs as examples of orderly activity attributed to natural instinct.

He then distinguishes rational human beings. A human being can examine natural movements, approve or reject them, and direct action through judgment. External circumstances and stimuli may arrive without being chosen; the use made of them is a separate question.

The passage describes a sequence:
1. An external event or stimulus occurs.
2. It arouses an impulse or desire.
3. The rational mind considers and judges the impulse.
4. The person assents or refuses assent.
5. Action follows.

This is Origen's conceptual account, not an experimentally established psychological model. The spider and bee examples are his illustrations of instinctive activity, not modern biological evidence.

#### B. The will and divine provision — III.1.6 and later discussion

Origen appeals to Micah, Deuteronomy, Isaiah, the Psalms, and the teachings of Jesus to argue that commands and accountability have meaning in relation to human response.

He also discusses the claim that the faculty by which people will is itself received from God. The capacity is given; its use can be directed toward good or evil. This distinction appears in the discussion of the power of volition and its use.

#### C. Divine action and human participation — III.1.22

Origen addresses the apparent tension between Paul's potter-and-clay language in Romans 9 and the instruction in 2 Timothy 2:21 that a person should cleanse himself and become a vessel unto honour.

Origen does not treat the statements as contradictory. In the transmitted text, he argues that human power apart from God's knowledge does not itself bring progress, and that God's knowledge does not make human contribution irrelevant. Nor does he describe the will of God as the only factor while excluding human acts, desires, and intention.

This is a substantial attempt to hold together divine action and human participation. It must not be reduced either to independent human self-salvation or to the claim that human response is meaningless.

#### D. Textual transmission caution

The work is not preserved completely in its original Greek. Much of it survives through Rufinus's Latin translation. Rufinus's preface explicitly says that he omitted passages he judged to be interpolated or forged, or contrary to Origen's other positions and his own belief. Some Greek fragments and Greek-derived translation sections survive, but the entire original cannot be checked against Rufinus.

This is a material limitation, not a minor editorial detail. Claims about Origen must distinguish:
- what the surviving text says;
- whether a section is transmitted through Rufinus or a Greek witness;
- what Rufinus says about his editorial method;
- what remains uncertain about Origen's exact original wording.

### 5. Comparative findings — what the three writers actually argue

There is a family resemblance among these arguments, but no proof yet that they express one identical doctrine or a single line of direct borrowing.

## Fourth examination — the cited Scriptures in their own contexts

This stage tests the passages cited by Justin, Irenaeus, and Origen against their surrounding KJV context. The purpose is to distinguish the biblical text from each writer's interpretation. A passage that supports moral accountability does not automatically establish every later philosophical claim made in its name.

### 1. Justin Martyr — Deuteronomy 30:15–20 and Isaiah 1:10–20

**Primary biblical texts:**
- [Deuteronomy 30:1–20, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy+30&version=KJV)
- [Isaiah 1:1–20, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+1%3A1-20&version=KJV)
- [Justin, First Apology 43–44](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0126.htm)

**Deuteronomy 30:** The passage belongs to Moses' covenant address to Israel. The people are addressed in relation to the covenant, the commandments, obedience, life and death, blessing and cursing, and the land they are about to enter. Verses 15–16 set life and good against death and evil in the context of loving the LORD and keeping His commandments. Verse 19 calls heaven and earth as witnesses and says, “therefore choose life.”

Justin's chapter 44 describes Moses as speaking to “the man first created” and gives a compressed formulation about good and evil and choosing good. The KJV text of Deuteronomy 30 does not identify Adam as its audience. It addresses Israel. Justin's wording also combines the contrast in verse 15 with the choice stated in verse 19; it is not a verbatim quotation of either verse.

**Historical observation:** Justin applies the covenant passage to a general argument about human responsibility. That is his interpretive use of the text. It must not be confused with the passage's stated audience or represented as a direct quotation addressed to Adam.

**Isaiah 1:** Isaiah identifies the vision as concerning Judah and Jerusalem. Verses 10–17 condemn corrupt worship and injustice; verses 16–17 call for cleansing, cessation of evil, justice, and care for the oppressed. Verses 18–20 set the Lord's offer and warning before the rebellious people: willingness and obedience are contrasted with refusal and rebellion.

Justin's appeal to Isaiah is substantially recognizable as Isaiah 1:16–20. It supports his point that the prophetic message calls for repentance and obedience and announces consequences. Its immediate setting, however, is Judah's covenant unfaithfulness and social injustice, not an abstract treatise on the metaphysics of the will.

**Finding:** Justin uses Scripture to argue that foreknowledge does not make human conduct morally meaningless. His application is broader than the immediate historical audience of the cited passages. The record must preserve both facts.

### 2. Irenaeus — Matthew 23:37–39 and Romans 2:1–11

**Primary biblical texts:**
- [Matthew 23:29–39, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+23%3A29-39&version=KJV)
- [Romans 2:1–16, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans+2%3A1-16&version=KJV)
- [Irenaeus, Against Heresies IV.37](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103437.htm)

**Matthew 23:** Jesus addresses Jerusalem in a lament that follows His denunciation of scribes and Pharisees and their treatment of the prophets. The words “how often would I have gathered thy children together” and “ye would not” express a real contrast between the gathering desired and the refusal described. The surrounding verses speak of Jerusalem's violence against those sent to it and the resulting desolation.

Irenaeus reads this refusal as evidence that human beings are not compelled to obey. That is a recognizable inference from the language of willing and refusal. The passage itself is a lament and judgment concerning Jerusalem; it is not a systematic account of every dimension of human freedom.

**Romans 2:** Paul's argument begins by confronting the person who judges another while doing the same things. Verses 4–5 contrast God's goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering—which lead toward repentance—with a hard and impenitent heart. Verses 6–11 describe judgment according to deeds and God's impartiality.

Irenaeus quotes this material to argue that people receive good from God and are accountable for what they do with it. The passage supports the themes of divine patience, repentance, conduct, and judgment. It does not, by itself, answer every question about the origin of willingness, the operation of grace, or the power of a fallen person to respond.

**Finding:** Irenaeus joins Jesus' lament over Jerusalem with Paul's account of judgment. His argument is that divine goodness is given and human refusal is meaningful. The biblical texts support the presence of response and accountability; the full philosophical account remains Irenaeus's interpretation.

### 3. Origen — Romans 9:14–24 and 2 Timothy 2:14–26

**Primary biblical texts:**
- [Romans 9:14–24, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans+9%3A14-24&version=KJV)
- [2 Timothy 2:14–26, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Timothy+2%3A14-26&version=KJV)
- [Origen, On First Principles III.1.21–22, textual presentation and notes](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04123.htm)

**Romans 9:** Paul's argument concerns God's mercy, election, the hardening of Pharaoh, the Creator's authority, and the relation between the One who forms and the thing formed. The potter-and-clay language in verses 20–21 must be read with the surrounding argument, including verses 14–24. It cannot be reduced to a statement that human beings independently determine their final condition.

**2 Timothy 2:** The vessel illustration occurs within instruction about false teaching, departing from iniquity, and becoming useful for the Master's work. Verses 20–21 describe vessels of different uses and the person who purges himself to become a vessel unto honour. The surrounding passage also matters: verse 25 says that God may grant repentance, and verse 26 describes escape from the snare of the devil.

Origen places Romans 9 alongside 2 Timothy 2:20–21. He argues that the potter image does not make the Creator the source of each person's evil conduct, because Paul also speaks of cleansing, praise, blame, and judgment. He reads the person's cleansing as relevant to the vessel's resulting condition.

This is Origen's reconciliation of the passages. It is not a neutral restatement of Romans 9 or 2 Timothy 2. In particular, Origen's wider argument includes his own account of prior causes and the history of rational souls. Those additional teachings must be identified as Origen's, not silently attributed to Paul.

**Textual caution:** The examined English presentation of Origen III.1.21–22 is based on Rufinus's Latin transmission of Origen's lost Greek, with editorial and textual notes. It is useful evidence for the transmitted argument, but it does not give direct access to every original Greek word. Rufinus's role and the transmission limits remain part of the evidence.

**Finding:** Origen tries to preserve both divine creative authority and meaningful human accountability. His solution is a developed theological interpretation. The two Pauline passages must first be allowed to speak in their own contexts before Origen's synthesis is evaluated.

### 4. Comparison with the literal KJV foundation — Genesis 1–2

**Primary text:** [Genesis 1–2, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1-2&version=KJV)

The comparison must begin with what Genesis itself states:

- Genesis 1:26–28 gives humanity a created place and commission, including dominion over the living creatures. It does not state that humanity receives authority to redefine the Creator's good.

**What is present:** God's originating action, provision, command, permission, boundary, and stated consequence. Man is addressed and given an assigned place within the Garden.

**Important distinction:** The ability to respond to a command, the responsibility associated with that command, and authority to determine what is good are separate questions. Genesis 1–2 gives the standard and boundary from God. It does not state that Adam is the authority who establishes that standard.

The historical writers may be compared with this foundation, but their terminology cannot be imported into Genesis as though it were already present there.

## Fifth examination — Augustine: free choice, divided willing, and grace

This stage follows the recorded handoff into Augustine. It asks what he inherited from earlier Christian and philosophical discussions, what his own writings add, and how his account develops across his lifetime. Augustine must not be treated as though every work expresses one unchanged formulation.

### 1. The early work — *De libero arbitrio* (*On the Free Choice of the Will*)

**Primary and scholarly sources:**
- [Augustine, *On the Free Choice of the Will*, full text](https://www.sophiascriptura.com/fathers/augustine-libero-arbitrio/read/)
- [Augustine's own later review, *Retractions* I.9, in an English scholarly edition](https://student.uoh.edu.iq/admin/ebooks/57559-saint-augustine-bishop-of-hippo%2C-thomas-williams-%28translator%29-on-free-choice-of-the-will---hackett-pub-co-inc-%281993%29.pdf)
- [Tomoki Ishikawa, “Revisiting ‘propria uoluntas et liberum arbitrium’ in Augustine's De Libero Arbitrio” (2024)](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/tetsugaku/8/1/8_36/_html/-char/en)

Augustine later explains that he and Evodius began discussing the origin of evil while in Rome. Books II and III were completed later in Africa, after Augustine's ordination. He says the inquiry was directed against the Manichaean claim that evil came from an independent, co-eternal evil nature. The early work's central argument is that moral evil originates in the will's own misuse, not in God as Creator or in a second evil substance.

In Book I, especially I.11.21, Augustine and Evodius conclude that the mind becomes enslaved to desire through its own will and free choice. The Latin expression is *propria voluntas et liberum arbitrium*. The passage concerns the mind's turning away from the unchangeable good toward lower, changeable goods. Augustine is arguing that this culpable turning cannot be assigned to God or to a rival eternal substance.

Book II asks why God gave free choice if it can be misused. Augustine's answer is that right action would not be possible without it. Free choice is therefore treated as a good gift that can be used rightly or wrongly; its possibility of misuse does not make the gift itself evil.

**What this establishes:** In this early anti-Manichaean argument, Augustine makes the will's own action central to the origin of moral evil and to human responsibility. He does not describe the will as an evil substance or as something forced by God to sin.

**Limit:** This argument is not a complete account of the later Augustinian teaching on grace, original sin, or the fallen will. Augustine himself later says the early books did not set out to defend at length the grace by which God prepares the wills of the elect.

### 2. Augustine's own qualification — *Retractions* I.9

Augustine's later review of *On the Free Choice of the Will* is essential evidence because it tells us how he himself understood the scope of his earlier argument.

He says that the work was written against those who blamed the Creator for evil and proposed an unchangeable evil nature co-eternal with God. He then adds that the books did not discuss in a sustained way the grace by which God predestines His elect and prepares the wills of those who use their freedom of choice.

This qualification prevents two opposite errors:

- It is inaccurate to cite the early work as though Augustine had already supplied his entire mature account of grace.
- It is also inaccurate to claim that the early work simply denied divine grace. Augustine says the issue was not the subject being fully treated in those books.

The distinction between the work's stated purpose and the wider theology Augustine developed later must be preserved.

### 3. *Confessions* VIII — the divided will

**Primary text:**
- [Augustine, *Confessions*, Book VIII](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/110108.htm)

Augustine's autobiographical account gives a different kind of evidence: not a formal philosophical dialogue, but his own retrospective account of an experience of conflicting desire and decision.

In VIII.9.21, he describes the mind commanding itself to will and yet not willing entirely. He rejects the Manichaean explanation that conflicting wills must come from two opposing substances. In VIII.10, he argues that conflicting desires belong to one soul; the conflict does not establish two independent natures.

In VIII.5 and VIII.11–12, he describes habit, desire, and the inability to carry out what he recognizes as the better course. The garden episode ends with his reading of Romans 13:13–14 and his conversion.

**Observation:** Augustine distinguishes willing from willing wholeheartedly. A person may recognize and desire the good while remaining divided by established habits and competing attachments.

**Interpretive limit:** This is Augustine's theological and autobiographical interpretation of his own experience. It is not a controlled psychological experiment, and it should not be translated automatically into modern clinical or neuroscientific categories.

### 4. The developing account — from the 390s to the Pelagian controversy

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's study of Augustine identifies development in his account of freedom and grace. In his earlier anti-Manichaean works, Augustine emphasizes the will's responsibility for its choices. Around 396, in *Ad Simplicianum* I.2, his reading of Paul leads him to emphasize more strongly the priority of divine grace and the impairment of human willing under the fallen condition. The later Pelagian controversy makes the relationship between grace and free choice a central subject.

The chronology matters:

- **Early *On the Free Choice of the Will* (begun around 388; completed in stages):** explains moral evil without blaming God or positing an eternal evil substance.
- **Around 396, *Ad Simplicianum* I.2:** a significant development in Augustine's interpretation of election, grace, and the beginning of faith.
- **Around 397–400, *Confessions*:** narrates divided willing, habit, and the experience of conversion.
- **From the 410s onward:** the Pelagian controversy intensifies Augustine's examination of original sin, grace, and the will's ability to turn toward God.
- **426–427, *On Grace and Free Will*:** Augustine explicitly argues against both those who deny grace in order to defend free choice and those who deny free choice in order to defend grace.

This is a development in emphasis and argument, but it should not be flattened into a simple claim that Augustine first believed in free will and later rejected it. His mature position continues to affirm free choice while insisting that the will requires God's grace to be healed and enabled to love righteousness.

### 5. Mature statement — *On Grace and Free Will*

**Primary text:**
- [Augustine, *On Grace and Free Will*](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1510.htm)
- [Augustine, *On the Spirit and the Letter*, especially chapters 52 and 58](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1502.htm)

Augustine identifies the controversy that occasioned *On Grace and Free Will*: some monks defended free will in a way that denied the necessity of grace; others defended grace in a way that appeared to deny free will. Augustine rejects both positions.

In chapter 2 he argues from God's commands that human beings have free choice. Commands, warnings, and judgment are not meaningless. But the rest of the work insists that the ability to do good and to persevere is not an achievement independent of grace.

In *On the Spirit and the Letter* 52 Augustine says that grace does not abolish free will but establishes it: grace heals the will so that righteousness may be freely loved. In chapter 58 he describes free choice as a gift naturally assigned to the rational soul, while also saying that the will with which a person believes is received from God.

**Observation:** Augustine's mature argument is not “free will or grace.” It is that free choice is real, but the will's healing and right use depend upon divine grace. The will is not an independent source of its own restoration.

**Limit:** These are Augustine's theological claims. Their biblical validity and relation to GUA-W remain to be tested against the relevant scriptural passages, not accepted because Augustine states them.

The historical record sharpens the need to distinguish the existence of a capacity from its condition, use, and source of restoration. Augustine's mature position treats the will as real while denying that it heals or restores itself independently of grace. This is a question for comparison, not a new GUA-W conclusion.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

Augustine's thought develops across different controversies. His early work answers the Manichaean question of the origin of evil; *Confessions* VIII describes the divided will; his later works address the necessity of grace and the limits of fallen human willing. Reading only one period would produce an incomplete account.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A witness must read a writer across time, attend to the writer's own later review of earlier work, and distinguish historical development from contradiction. It must not select one quotation and make it stand for an entire lifetime of thought.

## Sixth examination — Augustine on divine initiative and human response

**Question:** In Augustine's mature interpretation of Romans 5, Romans 9, John 6, and Philippians 2, does God's initiating action precede and enable the human response?

This examination distinguishes (1) the wording and immediate argument of each KJV passage, (2) Augustine's historical interpretation, and (3) the comparison that may be made with the GUA-W foundation. Augustine's interpretation is evidence about Augustine; it is not automatically identical to Paul's or John's full intended meaning.

### 1. Romans 5 — the Adam–Christ contrast and the need for grace

**KJV passages:** Romans 5:5–11, 12–19, 20–21.

Paul's argument moves from the love of God shed abroad in believers' hearts by the Holy Ghost (5:5), to Christ's death for the ungodly and sinners (5:6–8), to reconciliation through Christ (5:9–11). He then contrasts the one man through whom sin and death entered with the one Man, Jesus Christ, through whom grace and the gift of righteousness abound (5:12–19). The chapter closes with grace reigning through righteousness unto eternal life (5:20–21).

**Augustine's reading:** In *On the Spirit and the Letter*, especially chapter 9, Augustine reads Romans 5 as establishing the shared human predicament in Adam and the saving remedy in Christ. The law can expose and even aggravate transgression; it cannot, by itself, heal the will. Augustine reads Romans 5:5 as locating the inward gift of divine love in the Holy Spirit, not in unaided human effort. In his anti-Pelagian works, he uses Romans 5:12–19 to argue that the human condition is not explained merely by imitation of Adam: humanity needs the grace of the Redeemer.

**What this supports:** In Augustine's mature framework, the human being does not first restore himself and then qualify for Christ. The need is prior; Christ's grace is the remedy. The Spirit's gift of love is inward and enabling.

**Limit:** Romans 5 is primarily Paul's Adam–Christ argument about sin, death, grace, justification, and life. It does not, by itself, spell out every stage in the chronology of an individual's first movement toward faith. Augustine's doctrine of inherited original sin and his detailed account of grace are theological interpretations built from this and other passages.

Primary texts:
- [Augustine, *On the Spirit and the Letter*, especially chapters 9 and 47](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1502.htm)
- [Augustine, *On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins*, Book I](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/15011.htm)
- [Augustine, *On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins*, Book III](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/15013.htm)

### 2. Romans 9 — election not grounded in a prior human merit

**KJV passages:** Romans 9:6–24, with particular attention to 9:10–18.

Paul discusses the children of promise, Jacob and Esau, God's purpose according to election, and the words: “So then it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy” (9:16, KJV). He then addresses the objection concerning God's justice and uses the potter-and-clay image (9:19–24).

**Augustine's development:** Augustine's earlier explanations could appeal to God's foreknowledge of faith. In *Ad Simplicianum* I.2 (around 396–397), his interpretation changes substantially. He concludes that election cannot rest on God's foreknowledge of a good human willing or believing that originates independently of grace; otherwise the decisive distinction between the elected and the unelected would rest on a human merit. He reads Paul's argument as excluding boasting and locating the origin of saving good in God's gratuitous mercy.

This is a major turning point in Augustine's theology. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's account of Augustine notes that he explicitly abandons his earlier foreknowledge-of-faith explanation in this work and treats even the beginning of faith as dependent on grace.

**What this supports:** For Augustine, God's gracious initiative is not merely a response to a faith that first arises independently in the human being. God's call and grace produce or enable the good willing by which the person responds.

**Limit:** Romans 9 is a difficult passage whose immediate context includes Israel, the promise, God's freedom, mercy, hardening, and the identity of the people of promise. Augustine's reading is a historically influential interpretation, not the only interpretation Christians have offered. The passage must not be reduced to a slogan about individual choice without examining Paul's whole argument.

Primary and scholarly sources:
- [Augustine, *To Simplicianus*, Book I, Question 2 — English translation and study lead](https://www.google.com/search?q=Augustine+To+Simplicianus+I.2+Romans+9+translation)
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Augustine](https://plato.sydney.edu.au/entries/augustine/)
- [Cambridge Companion to Augustine — discussion of the Romans 9 turn in *Ad Simplicianum*](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/PHS325/%28Cambridge%20Companions%20to%20Philosophy%29%20Eleonore%20Stump%2C%20Norman%20Kretzmann-The%20Cambridge%20Companion%20to%20Augustine%20-Cambridge%20University%20Press%20%282001%29.pdf)

### 3. John 6 — the Father draws, and the drawn person truly comes

**KJV passages:** John 6:35–45, especially 6:37, 6:44–45, and 6:65.

Jesus says that no man can come to Him except the Father draw him (6:44); He also says that everyone who has heard and learned of the Father comes to Him (6:45). The passage connects the Father's action, coming to Christ, belief, and resurrection.

**Augustine's reading:** In *Tractates on the Gospel of John*, Tractate 26, Augustine calls this “the excellence of grace” and says no one comes unless drawn. But he directly rejects the inference that the person is dragged to Christ against his will. Belief requires willingness. Augustine explains the drawing through inward teaching and delight: the Father draws by making the truth, righteousness, and life desirable to the heart. The person comes willingly because grace has moved the person's desire.

Augustine also distinguishes outward proclamation from inward illumination. A teacher may speak outwardly, but understanding is given inwardly by God. He uses the planting-and-watering analogy of 1 Corinthians 3:6–7 to distinguish human proclamation from God's giving of the increase.

**What this supports:** God's drawing precedes and enables coming; the human act of coming remains a real act of willing. In Augustine's account, grace does not bypass the will but changes the heart's attraction so that the person comes willingly.

**Limit:** Augustine's explanation of “draw” as delight is his interpretation. John 6 itself says the Father draws and teaches; the precise mechanics of that drawing are not exhaustively described in the passage. Augustine's account should be identified as interpretation, not inserted into the verse as though every explanatory detail were explicit there.

Primary text:
- [Augustine, *Tractates on the Gospel of John*, Tractate 26](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1701026.htm)

### 4. Philippians 2 — human working and God's prior inward working

**KJV passage:** Philippians 2:12–13.

Paul tells the Philippians to “work out your own salvation with fear and trembling” (2:12), immediately grounding this exhortation in the statement that “it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure” (2:13).

**Augustine's reading:** In *On Grace and Free Will*, chapter 2, Augustine treats both verses as necessary. The command to work addresses human free choice; the warning against boasting follows because God works in believers both to will and to do. Augustine distinguishes God's operation that brings about willing from His cooperation with the person who is willing and acting. His memorable formulation is that God works in us without us in order that we may will; when we will, He works with us.

This is not a claim that the person does not will or act. It is a claim that the good will and its good action are not self-originating or self-sustaining apart from grace.

**What this supports:** Of the four passages, Philippians 2:12–13 gives Augustine his clearest explicit pairing of human responsibility with God's inward action in the will and deed. Divine initiative and human action are not presented as mutually exclusive causes operating at the same level.

**Limit:** Augustine's distinction between “operating” and “cooperating” is his theological explanation of Paul's two verses. The text itself states both the human exhortation and God's working; it does not use Augustine's later technical distinction in those words.

Primary texts:
- [Augustine, *On Grace and Free Will*, especially chapters 2 and 17](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1510.htm)
- [Augustine, *On the Grace of Christ and Original Sin*, Book I, chapter 5](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/15061.htm)

### 5. Comparison across the four passages

| Passage | Biblical emphasis | Augustine's mature interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Romans 5 | Sin and death through Adam; grace, righteousness, and life through Christ | Humanity needs the remedy it cannot provide for itself; grace heals and gives the Spirit's love |
| Romans 9 | God's purpose, election, mercy, and freedom | Election is gratuitous; even the good beginning of faith is not a prior human merit |
| John 6 | The Father draws and teaches; the person comes to Christ | Grace inwardly draws through teaching and delight, so the person comes willingly |
| Philippians 2 | Believers work; God works in them to will and to do | God operates to bring about good willing and cooperates in the willing person's action |

### 6. Answer to the central question

**Within Augustine's mature theology: yes. God's initiating action precedes and enables the good human response.**

That answer needs three qualifications:

1. **The response remains human.** Augustine does not say that a person believes, loves, or obeys without willing. John 6 is especially important here: the drawn person comes willingly.
2. **The good response is not an independent first cause.** Augustine rejects the idea that the decisive good will or first saving faith originates autonomously and then causes God to grant grace. His reading of Romans 9 and Philippians 2 places the source of good willing in God's action.
3. **Grace is inward, not merely external information or opportunity.** Augustine's interpretation of John 6 and Romans 5 understands grace as teaching, healing, and giving love within the person—not simply presenting a command and leaving an unchanged will to supply the good response unaided.

A concise statement of Augustine's position is therefore:

**God initiates and enables; the human being truly wills and responds; the good response remains dependent on the grace that brought it forth.**

This is Augustine's mature theological synthesis. It must not be confused with a direct quotation from any one of the four passages, nor treated as the only possible reading of them.

### 7. Comparison with the Genesis 1–2 foundation and GUA-W

The KJV Genesis 1–2 account establishes God as Creator and initiator: God speaks, makes, blesses, provides, places the man, gives work, and establishes the command and boundary. The man receives his existence, place, provision, and instruction. The text does not describe man as the originating source of his own existence or as the authority who establishes the command.

That is a direct textual observation. It is not yet a complete doctrine of post-Fall grace, nor does Genesis 1–2 itself explicitly describe Augustine's account of the inward healing of the will.

The comparison raises a relevant distinction without adding an architectural node:

- **Origin:** God is the Creator and giver.
- **Reception:** the created human being receives life, provision, place, and command.
- **Response:** the human being acts within the received order and is accountable to the command.
- **Restoration:** Augustine argues from later Scripture that fallen willing requires God's gracious initiative and healing.

The fourth item is Augustine's interpretation of later biblical texts. It must remain distinct from what Genesis 1–2 explicitly states.

No new GUA-W structure is proposed. The investigation records a possible correspondence between the Source's initiating action and the derivative recipient's dependent response, while leaving the full theological placement open.

His interpretation must still be distinguished from the full range of possible readings of Paul and John.
